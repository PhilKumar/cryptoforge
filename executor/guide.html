What this actually is

We work out the trade. Your computer places it.

We watch the market and work out where to buy and where to sell. That is our side, and it is all our side does.

This little program is your side. It sits on your computer, listens to what we worked out, and places the orders on your own exchange account — in your name, with your money, under your control.

Three things worth knowing up front

No borrowing. This buys coins with money you already have. There is no margin call and no liquidation. The worst case is holding a coin worth less than you paid.

It runs on your computer, not ours. While it is off, it is not doing anything. There is a section below on exactly what that means.

You can stop it any time, with one button, and it will never walk away from something you already own.

First, the important bit

One unticked box is most of your safety.

At some point you will create something on your exchange called an API key. It sounds alarming. It is not, if you set it up the way below.

Think of it as a spare car key that starts the engine but does not open the boot. It lets this program buy and sell on your account — and nothing else.

When your exchange asks what the key is allowed to do:

Spot trading
Tick this. It is what the program does.
Withdrawals
Leave off. Always. For anything.
Futures, margin, lending
Leave off. The program never uses them.

A key that cannot withdraw cannot take money out of your account. Not by us, not by a thief, not by a bug in anyone's software. That one box costs you nothing and removes the only genuinely bad outcome.

Nine steps, in order

Setting it up

Each step is one line you paste and one thing you should see back. If a step does not look right, stop there — the next one will not fix it.

1

Open the black window

Everything happens in a program already on your computer, where you type a line and press Enter.

On a Mac: press Cmd + Space, type Terminal, press Enter.
On Windows: click Start, type PowerShell, press Enter.

5

Create your ID and send it to us

Terminal

XCmGM01hy/L0EN8qqmpQ+GID4IMG9p2Upu67jXIR4ZQ=

Copy that line of nonsense and email it to us, with the name you would like to be known by.

What just happened: your computer made itself a pair of matching keys, like a lock and its key. It kept the private one and printed the public one. The public one is what you just sent — it is how we recognise you, and it is useless to anybody else. The private one never leaves your computer, and we never have it, so there is nothing on our side that could be stolen and used to pretend to be you.

We will email you back two things: a short name (your buyer ID) and another line of nonsense (the root key). You need both for the next step, so wait for that email before carrying on.

Make yourself a settings file, then open it:

A file opens. Change the parts to the right of each colon, keeping the quote marks exactly where they are:

config.json
{ "server_url": "https://crypto.philforge.in", "buyer_id": "buyer-your-name", "root_public_key": "(the long line we emailed you)", "exchange": "binance", "capital_usd": 3000, "symbols": [], "tick_seconds": 20 }
LineWhat to put
server_urlLeave exactly as it is.
buyer_idThe short name we emailed you.
root_public_keyThe long line we emailed you. This is how your computer checks a signal really came from us, and not from someone pretending to be us.

About that capital figure. It is not a safety limit you probably will not reach. It is the number every order size is worked out from. Put 3000 and it trades as though it has $3,000. Put your real figure.

7

Give it your exchange keys

On your exchange, create an API key with trading ticked and withdrawals not ticked — the section above. Copy the two long strings it gives you, usually called the key and the secret.

Open your settings file again and add them as two extra lines just before the closing brace. Mind the comma at the end of the line above them:

config.json
"tick_seconds": 20, "api_key": "paste the key here", "api_secret": "paste the secret here" }

From now on that file has your exchange keys in it. Do not email it, do not paste it into a chat, do not screenshot it.

If you ever need to send us something to sort out a problem, use step 8 instead — it prints the same information with the secret parts blanked out, safe to send to anyone.

8

Test it, without trading anything

Terminal

This buys nothing and sells nothing. It checks the three things that have to be right, and tells you about all of them at once so you are not fixing one problem at a time. If one says FAIL:

It saysAlmost always means

What it is actually doing

It buys a fall in steps, not in one go.

When a fall begins, the program does not buy at the top of it. It works out three prices on the way down and puts a portion of the money at each one — a little at the first, more at the second, most at the deepest. If the price only reaches the first step, that is all it buys.

A real set of levels, from a real fall. Buying lower three times pulls your average down to 156.69 — so the price only has to come back a quarter of the way for the round to close.

That is the whole idea: your average price improves as the fall deepens, so the price does not have to recover all the way for you to be out. The sell order goes in automatically as soon as there is something to protect, and it is a real order sitting at your exchange — not a reminder on your screen.

The minimum

Why it asks for $1,000.

Exchanges refuse orders below a minimum size — often about $5 to $10, depending on the coin. Each of those three steps is a separate order, and the first one is the smallest.

So below a certain point, the early steps come out under the exchange's minimum and get rejected, and you only ever get the deepest one. That is a completely different strategy from the one you are paying for.

With $3,000 behind it all three steps go in
step 1$24
step 2$36
step 3$60
With $1,000 behind it the first step gets skipped
step 1$8

step 1$4

step 3$10
the exchange will not accept an order under about $11
Example figures for one fall. The proportions are what matter: every step scales straight up and down with the money behind it, so halving your capital halves every bar.

Between $1,000 and $3,000 it works, but on smaller falls some early steps still land under the minimum, so it buys in fewer and deeper chunks than the signal describes. Your dashboard marks those with a coarse tag so you always know which ones. Above $3,000, every step lands as intended.

Going away

Closing the lid.

If you shut it down properly — click into the black window and press Ctrl + C, or press Stand down first — it cancels the waiting buy orders, makes sure everything you own has a sell order protecting it, and writes down where it got to. Give it a few seconds to finish.

If you just close the laptop lid, your computer gives the program a few seconds' warning before it sleeps, and it spends them doing that same tidy-up. How many seconds depends on the computer, and it is worth knowing which one you have:

Mac
~30 seconds
Linux
~5 seconds
Windows
~2 seconds
Plenty · enough · not enough.

On a Mac there is time to spare, and it also quietly stops your Mac dozing off on its own while an order is waiting — and stops doing that the moment nothing is waiting, so it never keeps your laptop awake for no reason.

On Windows, two seconds is not enough to cancel an order and be sure it worked. So treat that big number on your dashboard as the real thing, and press Stand down before you close the lid.

No software can stop a laptop lid from closing. What this program gets is a few seconds to react — never a veto — so it is built around using those seconds well, not around keeping your computer awake.

When you come back, it asks your exchange what actually happened while it was away, puts a sell order on anything you turn out to be holding, and shows you what changed. It will not start anything new until you click "I've reviewed — resume trading". That is deliberate: a program that has been away does not get to guess.

If you have no computer to leave on

Rent one that never sleeps.

Everything above assumes you have a machine that can stay switched on. Plenty of people do not — you work off a laptop, it goes in a bag, and that is that.

The answer is to rent a small computer that lives in a data centre and never closes. It costs about $5 a month, it belongs to you, and you install this program on it exactly the way you would on your own machine. Everyone calls these a VPS.

This changes nothing about who holds your money. Your exchange keys sit on a computer you rent and only you can log into. They still never reach us — that is the whole design, and renting the machine does not bend it.

Two things to do anyway, because a small machine fails differently from a big one:

Give it some swap. A half-gigabyte machine with no swap does not slow down when something spikes — it kills whatever is running. One command at setup turns a hard stop into a slow moment:

Terminal
sudo fallocate -l 2G /swapfile && sudo chmod 600 /swapfile
sudo mkswap /swapfile && sudo swapon /swapfile
echo '/swapfile none swap sw 0 0' | sudo tee -a /etc/fstab

Run nothing else on it. This is the real risk, and it is not this program being too big — it is a neighbour. One other busy thing on a small machine is what starves it. Keep this one dedicated and it will sit there quietly for years.

Watching it from your phone

Your phone is a screen, not the machine.

It cannot run there. Phones shut background programs down to save battery, and something placing real orders cannot be at the mercy of that. So the rented computer does the work — but your phone is a perfectly good way to watch it, and to stop it.

The dashboard is already built for a phone screen. And it is not just something to look at: Stand down, Pause and Resume are all right there, so you can pull every waiting buy order while you are standing in a queue somewhere.

Do not put the dashboard on the internet. It has a Stand down button and shows everything you hold. It is built to refuse anything that is not the machine it runs on, and that is worth keeping.

Instead, put your phone and the rented computer on the same private network, so they can see each other and nobody else can. A free tool called Tailscale does this — install it on both, sign into the same account, then on the computer run:

Terminal
tailscale serve 7757
Available within your tailnet: https://your-machine.tail-scale.ts.net/ Serving http://127.0.0.1:7757

Open that address on your phone and the dashboard is there. No port to open, no password that could leak, no public web address for anyone to find. The program still only answers to its own machine — Tailscale is what carries your phone into the same room.

Honestly: this path is longer than the twenty minutes on the front of this guide. Renting the machine, installing this program on it and adding your phone is closer to forty if you have never used a terminal. It is still once, and it is still every step written out — but we would rather you heard that number from us than discovered it halfway through.

What we can see about you

Three things, and that is the list.

Your public line of nonsense from step 5. When your program connects and disconnects. Whether you have paid.

We do not have your exchange keys, so we cannot see your balance or your trades. We never receive what you bought or sold, so we do not know whether you are up or down. There is no place in the signal we send you for any of that to travel — which is enforced by our software, not just promised on this page.

The honest version

The risk, stated plainly.

This program trades from your computer. If your computer stops, an order that was waiting may still go through, and you would own that coin until your computer comes back.

It cannot be sold out from under you — you are buying with your own money, not borrowed — and the most that can happen unwatched is the one order shown on your dashboard. That risk is small, and bounded, and it is not zero. Anyone who tells you their trading software has no such gap is either not thinking about it, or not telling you.
